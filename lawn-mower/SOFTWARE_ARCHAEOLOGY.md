# The software archaeology behind Lawn Mower

This is the recovery story of this one game. The philosophy shared by every game in the collection (preserve the reasoning and not just the artifact, verify by execution rather than resemblance, record provenance instead of erasing it) is in [`../SOFTWARE_ARCHAEOLOGY.md`](../SOFTWARE_ARCHAEOLOGY.md).

## A game that lives in 24 KiB

Lawn Mower is a game for the Nintendo Entertainment System, written in 6502 assembly by Shiru in May 2011 in about three weeks, for the NintendoAge NES Coding Competition 2011. His development notes, kept in the competition's archive, say how it came about: "Lan Master" was nearly finished, three weeks remained before the deadline, and he thought he could make "an extra, very simple and small game", a more or less exact clone of a lawn mower game of 1986 that he had seen on the BK-0010 and the ZX Spectrum. The idea did not stay small. "8x8 chars are difficult to see", "larger chars means smaller levels", "it is boring without music": at some point he settled on a 24 KiB cartridge, and not more, because the project had grown too big for the original idea.

Even so, the data did not fit, so the cartridge does two odd things. The ten levels are stored in the graphics memory (the character ROM), in the space the patterns leave free, as 95-byte records that the program reads through the picture unit's data port. And all the music shares one table of instruments. "There are about 180 bytes of free room in the PRG ROM." The notes also say that his assembler (NESASM 3.1) "does not report ROM overflow in some cases, producing not working ROM without any warnings", that the palettes of the emulators differ so much that one colour can vary "from lime to clean yellow", that a tester with a real console (B00daW) found a scrolling problem that seven emulators had hidden, "solved by altering design", and that the game does not run in two emulators of the time.

The author published the game, the ROM, a manual and a label on the competition's page, and later the source and the tools: the assembler files, the FamiTracker projects of the music, and a level editor written in Borland C++. The source carries a line that says what it is: "The game and its source code are released into Public Domain".

## What the cartridge does

The game is a single screen of 16 by 16 pixel tiles that scrolls sideways when the lawn is wider than 14 tiles (the ten lawns are 14, 20, 25 or 30 tiles wide and 11 high). The mower's position is a 16-bit number in 12:4 fixed point: the high byte is the tile. Every NMI is one step of the game, but the game was written for a 50 Hz machine and runs on a 60 Hz one, so a counter skips the logic of every fifth NMI (the comment in the source says "every 6th frame"; the code skips at 5). Some work, like the countdown to the next fuel can and the sound, goes on at every NMI.

A lawn is cut by driving over it: each uncut tile that the mower enters turns into one of four cut variants chosen by the random generator, and the cut counter and the percentage on the screen go up by a step that depends on the lawn. The fuel falls by one on every odd NMI that the game processes; a flower costs a sixth of a tank and a stone a third; a can fills it. A can appears when a countdown (a base of 150 to 250 for the level, plus a random 0 to 31) reaches zero, on a place of cut grass that the game has been choosing in advance, a candidate per NMI, by rejecting the places that are not cut. The random generator is eight bits, a shift register with the polynomial $CF, seeded with 1 when the console starts: the title screen draws numbers from it while it waits, so how long you stay on the title decides the lawns' look and the cans' places.

The screen is split in two at line 33. A sprite placed there waits for the "sprite 0 hit": the game polls the picture unit's status until the flag rises, and then changes the scroll, so the status bar stays still and the lawn scrolls. The title does the same twice more and splits into five bands. The message that says that the level is cleared or that the fuel is out is written into the map (three rows of large tiles, twice over the 32 columns) and drawn one column at a time; while it waits, a timed loop switches the screen to the other half of the map for a band in the middle, which makes it flash. A fade, in the end, is a table of 256 brightnesses applied to half of the palette on every NMI.

There is one thing the game does not say anywhere. On the title screen, RIGHT, UP, LEFT, LEFT switches on a level skip, with a sound, and then SELECT fills the tank, ends the wait at the start of a level and completes the lawn.

## Two versions of the same game

The ROM of the competition says "Lawn Mower v1.1 30.05.11". The source in the archive says "Lawn Mower v1.12 01.07.11": the author tidied it a month later. The restoration package that this port started from had compared them by reading and had found one place where the game differs: in the path of the pad routine that runs when the level is cleared or the tank is empty, v1.1 clears the requested direction, and v1.12 does not. Running both here shows the consequence: a mower that is moving at that moment reaches the edge of its tile in both, and there v1.1, with no direction left, stops, while v1.12 starts into the next tile until the end of the level takes over. They end up apart by less than a pixel (at most 14 sixteenths, measured with the port set to follow each version).

The competition ROM is the author's binary of the contest. The v1.12 source cannot be turned into its binary here: the assembler is not in the archive. A small assembler for the subset of NESASM that the source uses, ported from the one the package had (which was in Python and could not be run), builds the source into ROMs that are the package's two verification ROMs byte for byte. They are not the author's binary and are not presented as one: a later download proved it, because the Internet Archive's `lawn_mower.zip` holds the author's own build of v1.12, which says `v1.12 01.07.11` like the source but differs from the assembled ROM in 7,013 of the 16,384 bytes of program. So the port is compared with three programs: the competition ROM, the author's v1.12 ROM, and the source assembled here, with the one switch that makes it follow v1.1. All three agree with it, apart from one set-up that lasts a frame longer in the author's v1.12 ROM.

That was true when the assembler was written, and it is not true of every assembler: on 18 October 2026 the assembler written for Lan Master, which knows that NESASM encodes an operand as zero page only when it is a plain number of one or two digits or is written with `<`, assembled this same source into the author's v1.12 ROM with no byte different. The older assembler here lacks that rule, which is why its program is laid out differently from his; the oracle's third program is still that one.

## What the restoration package could and could not show

The package is the record of nine milestones and six release candidates. It carries a game kernel in JavaScript, with a "behavioural oracle" of its own: a Python script that computed what it believed the rules were and compared the kernel with that, and a series of renderings of its drawing code whose hashes its tests pinned. It could not run the cartridge. So its tests compared the port with the package's own reading of the source: where the reading was right they agreed, and where it was not they could not tell.

## What running the original showed

There was no 6502 compiler and no emulator in this environment, so one was written, as for the collection's other cartridge ports: a 6502 core with cycle counts, a minimal NES with the memory, the controller ports, the vertical blank and the NMI, and for this game the sprite 0 hit, which the game polls to split the screen. The competition ROM boots on it in 21 frames and shows its title. The game's variables are at fixed addresses, written at the top of `game.asm`, so nothing has to be searched for in the code. A scripted player drives the ROM and the port with the same buttons, and at every NMI their variables are compared.

The first run stopped three steps into the first level. The differences, one after another, were these:

- **The skipped NMI that runs the game.** Every fifth NMI is skipped by returning to the loop's start with X set to zero, and X is where the loop keeps whether this NMI is to be processed. The routine that looks for a place for the next fuel can loads X with 11 and does not put it back, so a skipped NMI in which that search runs is not skipped: the game logic runs, and the delay counters advance one step early. It happened three steps into the first level, when the search finds its first place. Nothing in the text of the source says it.
- **The borders of the lawn.** The package's map drew the sky and the hedge across the whole width and plain hedge at the corners. The routine draws the sky as rows 0 and 1, the hedge as row 2 and row 14, the sides down rows 3 to 14 with four corner tiles over them, and then clears everything to the right of the lawn in rows 0 to 13 to blank.
- **What a level starts with.** The routine that starts a level looks at the tile under the mower once (usually for the "nothing here" mute on the grass sound's channel), and leaves two fuel coordinates as they were.
- **The pad.** The package passed the page's START as a one-shot event. The game reads the pad in only some of its loops (not during the wait before play, not while the mower is stopped for the end of a level, not during the fade of a message), treats a button as pressed when it was not down at its previous read, and un-pauses or acknowledges the final screen only when START is the one new button. The previous read of the pad can be a whole level ago.
- **The cheat.** Not in the package at all.
- **The frames the screen is off.** Between screens the display is off for 10 NMIs (levels 1 to 3), 9 (the others), 1 (the final screen) or 2 (the title that follows it), while the game does its setting up, and the frame counter goes on counting. Two rules read its low bit (the fuel falls on odd NMIs, and the speed decays an extra step on even ones), and the can blinks with another of its bits, so a counter that does not count those frames drifts from the original's by 9 or 10 with every level, and with it the NMIs on which the fuel falls. The lengths are the time of the setting-up code in cycles, and the machine measured them: the three narrow lawns (14 tiles wide) keep the display off for 9.2 to 9.3 frames' worth of cycles and the others for 8.3, because the loop that clears the ground to the right of the lawn has more to clear when the lawn is narrow; the work starts about two thirds of the way into an NMI, so that is 10 NMIs and 9.
- **The messages.** The message is written twice over the map and drawn one column per processed NMI; the second half is blanked as it goes, and also on the NMIs that the pacing skips; the mower's sprites are taken off the screen as it passes over them; while it waits, the game redraws rows 5 to 7 of one column on every NMI (the code was probably meant to draw 7 to 9), and the flashing band is lines 118 to 155 (the package had put it two lines higher).

Then the machine's own memory was compared as well: the sprite page, the two nametables, the palette. The sprites turned out to have a priority: where two sprites overlap the one with the lower number is in front, and the mower's body, the second palette's sprites over the first's in the package's painting, had lost its details. The palette of the original fades through a brightness table, with the background half updated on even NMIs and the sprite half on odd ones, so that during a fade the two halves are one step apart; the package dimmed the whole picture with 36, 68 and 100 per cent of black.

## What the pictures and the sound showed

The picture that the ROM puts on the screen is composed from its memories and from a log of its writes to the picture unit's registers, with the CPU cycle each fell on, and compared with the port's pixel by pixel. The compositor knows the one rule that matters for this game: the coarse horizontal scroll of a line is what was written before the end of the line before; a write in the middle of a line (the status bar's split at line 33, the title's four, the message's three) changes the rest of that line at a dot that depends on how the CPU and the picture unit were aligned, so those lines are left out of the comparison and reported. Everything else, 240 minus a handful of lines, is equal.

The sound is compared as the 11 bytes that the FamiTone player hands to the sound chip. The first run of the package's player stopped on the 12th NMI of the title: a silent triangle channel keeps its last pitch in the buffer in the original, and the package zeroed it. The rest, one after another: the idle effect streams, whose cleared buffers the package mixed over the music; the mute, which is effect number 10 and is mixed once; the level-clear mute, which comes ten counts after the tune stops and not at once; the can's sound, which is heard in the NMI that adds it, because the routine that adds it runs before the sound player; and the "skip" sound the cheat plays. Every tune is played past its loop (the title's takes 3,800 frames, the game's 3,860, the messages' 544, the final screen's 2,560) and every effect is started.

## What it is not

The comparison is exact on the game, on the picture unit's memories, on the sound player's output and, but for those few lines, on the picture. It does not cover what the page does with them: the colours (a table chosen for this port; the console's vary), how the 11 bytes become Web Audio, the keys and touch controls, the panel. The author's manual and label were not read for their licence (the manual's text is in embedded fonts) and are not redistributed. The competition archive is byte for byte the copy that PDRoms, a third-party host, has offered since June 2011 (checked on 18 October 2026), and was not compared with the author's own server. These and the others are listed in [`PROVENANCE.md`](PROVENANCE.md).
