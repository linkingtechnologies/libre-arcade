# The software archaeology behind Zooming Secretary

This is the recovery story of this one game. The philosophy shared by every game in the collection (preserve the reasoning and not just the artifact, verify by execution rather than resemblance, record provenance instead of erasing it) is in [`../SOFTWARE_ARCHAEOLOGY.md`](../SOFTWARE_ARCHAEOLOGY.md).

## A small game that took half a year

Zooming Secretary is a game for the Nintendo Entertainment System by Shiru and Alexei "PinWizz" Bespalko, released on 28 December 2011 and fixed two days later (v1.02). Shiru's development notes say how it began: the two had a larger NES game that was going slowly, and he proposed a small one first, in a week or so, with the feel of Game & Watch and the early Nintendo arcade games, to finish a project for once. The week became half a year, with long pauses. The idea of a secretary and telephones was his first random one, and it stayed. Unlike his earlier NES games it is not a clone or a remake.

The program is written in C, with more than 2,600 lines by hand and about 1,400 of sprite definitions, animations and palettes, compiled with cc65 for an NROM cartridge (32 KiB of program). It ran out of room near the end, when the bonus level and the text messages were still missing, and the notes list what was cut to stay without a mapper: support for several characters at once (3 KiB, unused by the game), a brightness table, sample playback, and some rewriting, including the replacement of post-increments by pre-increments where possible, which saved more than 700 bytes.

## What the restoration package had

A restoration package, `zooming-secretary-1.0.0-rc9-m32-source.zip`, came with the request. It held a JavaScript port with a page around it, the layouts of the eight stages as data, 32 reports and 24 test runners with 349 cases. The port was not a transcription. It was a reimplementation written from a reading of the source and the ROM, with a new renderer and new sounds, and it was careful: it had the numbers, the phones' lifecycle, the characters' behaviour. Its tests compared the port with what its authors had read, and with traces of the ROM taken by a tracer that was not in the package. Those traces could not be repeated, so none of the claims in the reports, that the core was frozen and certified, could be checked by anyone holding the package.

That is level 3 of the collection's verification hierarchy, careful reading, and it can say a port is consistent with its authors' understanding of the source. For a program compiled by cc65 that is not the same as agreeing with the cartridge.

## The oracle

The original's ROM, with its hash, was one of the things the package named. The pictures and the music were not needed, but the rules were. The ROM and the source were fetched on the day of the integration, with the integrator's permission, from a third party's copy (an emulator project's repository) and then checked against another (the Internet Archive's, whose release archive has the hash the package had recorded). They are not kept in this repository, nor is any other file of the original (`reference/` holds a record and hashes). `npm test` does not need it.

To run it, the 6502 core and the NROM board of Lawn Mower's oracle (which started from Concentration Room's) were reused, and the rest was written for this game:

- **Finding the state.** The source declares its variables as `static` with no initialiser, and cc65 puts those in the order they are declared, from $0300. The table of where each variable lives is that order, anchored on the one array that can be found in the running program without the source: the 704-byte map the program fills from its screen. Every variable that is compared was seen to behave as the source says.
- **Reaching a stage.** The program's `main` writes the level and the allowance of missed calls just before it plays; the oracle writes the values it wants in the same places, and at the start of the loop sets the random generator's byte. The stage's set-up, with all its random numbers, is the program's own code.
- **One iteration.** The program waits for a frame, reads the pad, runs its logic and loops. The oracle runs frames until the program has come to the top of its loop again, and reads the state it had there (a paused game goes round the loop without counting frames). On a 60 Hz console the library skips one frame in six, so about one iteration in five takes two frames, and the oracle counts iterations.
- **Players.** One presses nothing, one holds random directions, and one answers the calls: it plans a path with the port's own movement code, to the cabinet of the topic that will be missed first and then to the phone. A player looks at the port's state and never at the ROM's; the same buttons go to both. Any of them can also press START now and then, which is the program's pause.
- **Campaigns.** The oracle can also let the ROM run its own screens between stages, so that the stage that follows, the allowance and the generator's byte are the program's own.
- **The front end.** The ROM is also started from reset and run one pass of its loops at a time, next to a model of the title screen, the sound test and the choice of the level, with buttons that press the hidden code right and wrong, open the sound test in several ways and choose levels; it is compared after every pass, and then in the stage that follows.
- **The recording.** The ROM's state after every iteration is written down as a checksum, so that `npm test` replays the buttons against the port without the ROM, and a test breaks the port in 19 ways to show that the replay notices each.

## What it found

The first thing the oracle said was that the package agreed with the ROM, in every stage and with no button pressed, for runs of up to 1,500 iterations, including the loss of a stage to missed calls. That covers the set-up of the stages, the phones' lifecycle and the random numbers, and it is a good deal. The differences started when a player moved.

- **The ladders.** In the source a climbing step does not end the iteration: when the 16-pixel segment has just run out the player looks for the next in the same iteration. The package left it for the next, so it lost an iteration in every segment of every ladder. And the flag that says the player is on a ladder is cleared only when a direction is pressed with no ladder to take, so a player who stops on a ladder cannot walk sideways until he goes on or falls.
- **A character at the left edge.** The source copies the position of ManBox and Dibrov into an unsigned byte before it tests contact. They leave the screen by the left at a position from -8 to -1; for that test they are at 248 to 255, so they touch someone standing at the right edge.
- **The hearts.** They do nothing to the game, but the routine that adds one takes two numbers from the shared random generator only when one of its eight slots is free. After Dibrov grants wisdom all eight are sometimes in use, and then it takes none. The package took two every time, so from there on every number was different.
- **The Chief.** The source tests how near he is with `(px-32)>=(player_px+16)`, and cc65 compiles that as a comparison of unsigned 16-bit numbers. Left of the 32nd column the difference wraps, the test is true, and he does not slow the player however close he is. It was found by putting the player of Day 2 beside him and then reading the disassembly of the ROM to see which operation was which.
- **The seeds.** The original never reseeds its random generator. Each stage starts from the byte the stage before it ended with, and so does a stage played again after a game over. The page started every stage but the first from 1, so that a lost stage was played again with exactly the same calls.

Two faults of the oracle itself were found in the same way, by a disagreement that was the measuring instrument's. After the last iteration of a stage the ROM leaves the loop without the increment at its top, which the way of stepping had not allowed for. And the program, which has one set of variables for whatever character a stage has (the notes say the support for several was cut), does not clear what a character does not use: the Geek of Day 5 starts with the Ghost's wait of 255 after the Dream was lost, so each character is compared on the fields the game reads for it.

## Checked against the cartridge

56 scenarios (the eight stages, three kinds of player, three rounds of seeds and allowances) 48 more of them with the bots pressing START, 47 front ends and 5 campaigns (two from the title screen to the ending, two from the Dream, one from Day 7) were run against the ROM. In all, 484,895 iterations, 9,698 seconds of game, and 14,082 passes of the front end, agreed in every iteration after the five corrections. The page was then driven in a real browser: a bot played the whole campaign in real time, and the layout was measured in two languages at ten window sizes.

## What the cartridge does not say

The title screen reads the pad while it waits for START. If the buttons pressed, one new button at a time, are B, A, B, A, left, up, B, A, the program makes a sound and from then on shows the number of the level before every stage and lets the player change it with left and right (from the first level to a ninth number that leads to the final screen) until START, A or B. Anything else pressed in between, or two buttons at once, and the code starts again. A second thing is hidden in the same screen: if A, B, SELECT and START were all down at the read in which START was pressed, the program opens a sound test after its 72-pass wait, where up and down choose an effect or a tune and B plays it. It looks at the pad as it was when START was pressed, not at the end of the wait, because it does not read the pad in between; a scenario that presses all four buttons only during the wait shows that the ROM ignores them. The pause is START in the game: the pass that reads it does nothing else, the loop goes round without counting frames and, after half a second, waits for START again, reading the pad at the top of the loop as the pass before it left it, so that a START held through the pause does not end it; after it ends the program takes no START for 24 passes.

None of it was in the package, whose page had a pause of its own. It was written from the source into `Simulation` and `public/src/frontend.js` and compared with the ROM, and the first comparisons agreed; the replay sees each of ten deliberate breaks. The page has them for the keyboard and for the touch pad (SELECT, B and A buttons) and does not mention them in its text.

## What stays open

The oracle covers the rules and the campaign. The pictures, the sounds, the texts, the touch controls and the pace on a real console are not compared with the original, because the page's are new work. The sound test plays the page's own sounds and not the original's, and the page has no music. A call answered by wisdom before any cabinet is not covered: in the source the program then reads past the end of an array, and the port does not. The ROM and the source files used are third parties' copies of the author's release; they agree with each other and with the package's record, but that they are the authors' own bytes is not established, because the author's page did not answer. The manual and the cartridge label carry the notice "CC BY 2011" and the manual calls the licence Creative Commons Attribution, and none of them says which version, so that remains unknown. The comparison is level 1 for the rules; the page is level 3. The package's own claim that its core was certified is not repeated here.
